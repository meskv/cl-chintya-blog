Every wave of automation arrives with the same fear attached: this time, it's coming for the clever work, the creative work, the work we thought was safely human. And every wave of automation, so far, has taken something else first — the work that was clearly defined, repeatable, and measurable, regardless of how much skill it required to do well.

Bank tellers, typesetters, elevator operators, toll collectors, switchboard operators: these were not unskilled jobs when automation arrived for them. Many required years of training and genuine dexterity. What they had in common wasn't a lack of skill. It was a lack of ambiguity — a clearly specifiable task with a clearly checkable output.

## Ambiguity, not intelligence, is the moat

The professions that have resisted automation longest are not necessarily the ones requiring the most raw intelligence. They're the ones where the task itself resists clean specification: where "done well" depends on context that shifts case by case, where the output has to be judged by someone who also can't fully articulate their own criteria in advance.

A radiologist reading a scan for a known pattern is, in this framework, closer to a toll collector than to a family doctor managing an ongoing, ambiguous relationship with a patient whose life keeps generating new, unspecifiable context. This is not a claim about which job is more prestigious. It's a claim about which job is more automatable, and the two turn out to be almost unrelated.

## Why the fear keeps pointing the wrong way

We tend to associate automation risk with the jobs that look impressive to lose, because the loss of a prestigious job makes for a better headline than the loss of a toll booth. But headlines have historically been a poor predictor of where automation lands next. The clean, well-specified core of many high-status jobs — drafting a standard contract clause, generating a first-pass diagnosis, summarising a known category of document — is exactly the part most exposed, while the messy, ambiguous, relationship-dependent remainder of that same job often survives intact.

## What to actually watch for

Not "is this job prestigious" but "how much of this job can be written down as a checklist without losing anything important." The gap between the checklist and the actual job — the part that resists being written down — is usually where the durable human work has been hiding all along, in every previous wave, and probably in this one too.
