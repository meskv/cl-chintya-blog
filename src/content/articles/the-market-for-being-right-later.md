Predict a crash every year for a decade and, on the one year it happens, you get to be the person who called it. The nine years you were wrong quietly disappear from the story, absorbed into the larger narrative of patience finally rewarded. This is not a flaw in how we talk about forecasters. It's closer to the entire business model.

## Asymmetric memory

Being wrong in a boring, uneventful way costs almost nothing in reputation — nobody was watching closely during the years nothing happened. Being wrong in an exciting way, by contrast, is expensive and memorable. This asymmetry pushes forecasters, even honest ones, toward predictions that are wrong quietly and right dramatically, because the two kinds of error are not judged on the same scale.

A forecaster who says "the market will be roughly flat this year" is right about as often as one who says "the market will crash this year," across a long enough time horizon of alternating years. But only one of them gets book deals when they turn out to be right at the moment it matters most.

## What this does to advice

If you are on the receiving end of forecasts — economic, political, or otherwise — the useful question is rarely "was this person right once." It's "what is this person's full track record, including the years nobody was counting." A forecaster's incentive is almost never aligned with giving you their honest, boring, most-likely-scenario estimate, because boring estimates that turn out correct earn no attention at all.

## A cheap correction

The fix is not to distrust forecasting altogether — some of it is genuinely useful — but to insist on seeing the full distribution of past calls, not the highlight reel. Anyone confident enough to have been right eventually should be comfortable showing you every year they were wrong in between. If they aren't, the eventual rightness was probably closer to luck finding a permanent home in a repeated bet than to any real insight arriving on schedule.
