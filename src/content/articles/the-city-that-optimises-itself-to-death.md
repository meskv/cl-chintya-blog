Every efficiency gain removes some slack from a system, and slack, it turns out, was doing more work than anyone budgeted for. A road with no spare capacity carries more cars, on average, than one built with margin — until a single stalled vehicle turns the entire corridor into a parking lot with no alternative route, because the alternative routes were optimised away years ago for carrying "unnecessary" redundant traffic.

Cities are now full of systems built on this same trade: dynamic pricing that fills every seat and every delivery slot, routing algorithms that eliminate the "wasted" gap between one errand and the next, sensor networks that flag any unused capacity as a target for the next optimisation pass. Each of these decisions is individually reasonable. Together, they produce a city with no give left in it.

## Slack was never waste

The old, unoptimised city had spare taxis idling, half-empty buses running anyway, restaurants with tables held for no one in particular. An efficiency-minded observer sees pure waste in this. A systems-minded observer sees the buffer that let the city absorb a surge, a strike, a storm, a large wedding, without falling over.

Removing that buffer doesn't make a system efficient. It makes it brittle, and hides the brittleness behind excellent performance right up until the day it doesn't.

## Who experiences the brittleness

The people who benefit most from a fully optimised city are rarely the ones who pay for its fragility. A logistics platform that runs its delivery fleet at 98% utilisation posts excellent quarterly numbers. The driver whose entire day collapses because one optimisation-driven routing decision left no room for a flat tyre pays the actual cost, alone, off the books.

This is the pattern worth noticing in almost every "smart city" pitch: the smartness is measured in aggregate throughput, and the fragility it creates is measured, if at all, in individual stories that never make it into the dashboard.

## What a resilient optimisation would look like

Not less technology — better-specified goals for it. A traffic system tuned to minimise worst-case delay behaves very differently from one tuned to minimise average delay, even though both are "optimised." A delivery network with a deliberate 10% slack built in will look inefficient in a spreadsheet and will still be running normally on the one day a year that matters.

The cities worth living in going forward will be the ones whose engineers were allowed to put "resilience" in the objective function, not just in the postmortem after the object function broke something.
