A common complaint about democratic process is that it is slow relative to the problems it faces: a climate crisis that moves in decades against a legislative cycle that moves in years interrupted by elections; a pandemic that spreads in days against a public health bureaucracy that moves in weeks. The comparison is accurate. It's also comparing two things that were never designed to run at the same speed, for reasons that were, at the time, entirely deliberate.

## Slowness was a feature against a different failure mode

Deliberative process — committees, hearings, courts, multiple readings of a bill — was built primarily to prevent a different kind of disaster: fast, reversible-looking decisions made by a small number of people that turn out to be irreversible in practice. The framers of most democratic systems were less worried about being too slow to respond to a crisis than about being fast enough to permanently damage something on a bad week.

Judged against that original design goal, the slowness isn't a bug report. It's closer to a specification being met exactly as intended, in a world that has since generated a new category of problem — the fast-moving global kind — that the specification never anticipated.

## Two different clocks, one system

The uncomfortable position democracies now occupy is running two failure modes at once: still vulnerable to the fast, ill-considered decision the slow process was built to prevent, while also facing genuinely fast-moving threats the slow process is structurally unable to meet in time. Neither speeding up nor slowing down the entire system addresses both problems, because they call for opposite adjustments.

## A more useful question than "should it be faster"

Rather than treating speed as a single dial to turn, it may be more productive to ask which categories of decision actually need the deliberative brakes, and which need a separate, faster track built for problems that behave like emergencies rather than policy questions. Many functioning democracies already do this informally — emergency powers, expedited procedures, standing crisis authorities — with predictably uneven results depending on how honestly "emergency" gets defined.

The design problem is not democracy versus speed. It's building the two-speed system honestly enough that the fast track doesn't quietly become the default route for anything a government finds inconvenient to deliberate about.
