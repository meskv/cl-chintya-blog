An institution is, at the moment of its founding, almost always a good idea. A grain reserve against famine. A court to settle disputes without blood. A central bank to stop currencies from being printed into worthlessness. Someone identified a recurring failure and built a standing structure to absorb it.

The trouble starts later, once the structure has outlived the failure it was built for, or the failure has changed shape while the structure has not. What was once a shield becomes a weight. And the people inside the institution — the ones drawing salaries from it, the ones whose careers are built inside its logic — rarely notice the shift, because from where they sit, the institution is still doing exactly what it was designed to do: exist.

## The founding problem is not the operating problem

Every institution has two problems: the one it was built to solve, and the one it must solve every year after that in order to keep existing. These are not the same problem, and an institution's incentives quietly migrate from the first to the second the moment its survival is no longer guaranteed by its usefulness alone.

A regulator created to prevent a specific kind of financial collapse will, decades later, still be staffed, funded, and busy — not necessarily because that kind of collapse remains likely, but because an entire professional ecosystem has grown up around preventing it. Careers, consultancies, and conferences now depend on the threat being taken seriously, whether or not it still is.

This is not corruption in any dramatic sense. It's closer to a design flaw: we build institutions to be durable, and durability, once achieved, doesn't know how to switch itself off.

## Sunset clauses are rare for a reason

The obvious fix — build institutions with expiry dates, force periodic re-justification — sounds simple until you try it. Every institution's own logic argues against its sunset, and it usually controls the process that would decide its sunset. Turkeys, as the saying goes, rarely vote for an early Christmas.

What tends to work instead is external pressure that the institution cannot absorb into its own survival narrative: a genuinely new crisis, a generational change in leadership, or a slow public loss of legitimacy that finally makes continuation more costly than reform. None of these are things an institution can schedule for itself.

## What this means for anyone building something new

If you are starting anything meant to last — a company, a foundation, a small local body — it is worth writing down, at the beginning, what would tell you it's time to fold or fundamentally re-found the thing. Not because you will honor it later. Almost no one does. But because the act of writing it down is the closest you can get to remembering, from inside the institution's own eventual self-interest, that it was built to serve a purpose outside itself.

The alternative is what we already have: a landscape of structures still standing long after the flood they were built to hold back has receded, quietly finding new floods to justify themselves against.
