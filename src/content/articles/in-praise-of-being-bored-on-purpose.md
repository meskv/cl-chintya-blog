There used to be a kind of boredom that arrived without being invited: waiting for a bus, sitting through a delayed flight, standing in a queue with nothing to read. It was mildly unpleasant and, in hindsight, doing something useful — it gave the mind unclaimed time to wander into whatever it actually wanted to think about, rather than whatever had been placed in front of it a moment before.

That kind of boredom is now nearly extinct, not because queues got shorter, but because every gap has a device-shaped filler ready before the gap has even fully opened. The unclaimed time has been claimed in advance.

## Boredom was doing real cognitive work

Psychologists who study mind-wandering have found that the unfocused mental state we call boredom is closely associated with a kind of background processing: connecting loosely related ideas, working through unresolved problems without direct attention, consolidating memory. It's not empty time. It's time the mind spends on tasks that direct attention is bad at.

Remove the boredom and you don't get more focused time — you get a person constantly context-switching between one piece of directed attention and another, with the background process that used to run during the gaps simply never starting.

## Reclaiming it feels like a discipline now

The strange part is that boredom, once a default state, now has to be actively defended, sometimes performed — leaving a phone at home, taking the "long way" on a walk, sitting on a train without headphones. What was once the absence of stimulation now requires deliberate effort, occasionally against a mild social pressure that treats an unoccupied mind as a wasted moment rather than a working one.

## A small, unglamorous suggestion

Not a retreat, not a detox week, not a new app to schedule boredom into your calendar — that would defeat the point. Just leave one gap a day unfilled on purpose. The bus stop, the kettle boiling, the elevator ride. Let it be boring. Whatever your mind does with that time, it's probably doing something you weren't going to get to on purpose.
