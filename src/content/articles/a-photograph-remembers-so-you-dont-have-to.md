There is a small, well-documented effect researchers call the photo-taking impairment effect: people who photograph an object or a moment tend to remember it worse than people who simply observed it. The camera doesn't just fail to help memory. It appears to actively substitute for it — the brain, sensing that the record has been externally offloaded, quietly declines to also store its own copy.

This shouldn't be surprising once you say it plainly. Memory is metabolically expensive and evolution built it to be selective, not exhaustive. Offer the brain a reliable external backup and it will, quite reasonably, stop duplicating the effort.

## The trade nobody negotiated

What makes this worth noticing is that nobody sat down and chose it. No one decided "I would like to remember this wedding, this sunset, this friend's face slightly less clearly, in exchange for having a slightly better copy of it stored on a device." The trade happened as a side effect of a much smaller decision, repeated thousands of times: it would be nice to have a photo of this.

Multiply that small decision across a life increasingly documented in real time, and you get a genuinely strange outcome: a generation with more visual records of their own lives than any before it, and by some measures, a thinner felt memory of the moments those records depict.

## Documentation is not the same as presence

The photograph and the memory are not competing for the same kind of storage, which is exactly the problem. A photograph preserves what a scene looked like. A memory — an actual, lived one — preserves something closer to what it felt like to be there, including details a camera was never pointed at. When the first substitutes for the second, we don't lose information. We lose a different kind of information than we think we're keeping.

## A modest adjustment, not a rejection

None of this argues for refusing to take photographs — a life with zero photographic record has its own losses. It argues for occasionally, deliberately, putting the camera down before the moment has fully happened, and trusting the more expensive, more fallible, more human system to do the one job the camera can't: deciding, without being told, what actually mattered enough to keep.
