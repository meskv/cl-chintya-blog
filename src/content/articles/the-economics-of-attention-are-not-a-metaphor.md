Call something an "economy" loosely enough and the word stops meaning anything. But the attention economy has always been stranger than the metaphor suggests, because unlike most markets, it doesn't trade a resource that exists independently of the transaction. Attention isn't mined or grown; it's generated in real time, inside a person, and spent the instant it's noticed.

This has a consequence that markets built on durable goods don't share: the "customer" and the "product" are frequently the same entity, and the good being sold was never owned by the seller in the first place. A newspaper sells advertising space; a platform sells you.

## Scarcity without a natural limit

Classical scarcity comes from the world: there is only so much wheat, so much land, so much time before winter. Attention scarcity is different — it comes from the architecture of a single human nervous system trying to process a supply of stimuli that has no natural ceiling at all. The market didn't discover a scarce resource and start pricing it. It built machinery specifically to make more of the resource exist to sell, and the resource happened to be made of the very people it was selling to.

This is closer to a fishery that manufactures more fish than the sea ever could, sells them, and calls the resulting depletion of the ocean an unrelated externality.

## What a "price" looks like here

In an attention market, price isn't paid in currency by the person whose attention is taken. It's paid in the currency of what that attention would otherwise have gone toward: a conversation, a walk without a destination, an unresolved thought given room to finish itself. These transactions don't appear on anyone's balance sheet, which is precisely why they are so easy to keep making.

## The honest version of the metaphor

If we took the economic framing seriously rather than decoratively, we would ask the questions we ask of every other market: who owns the resource being extracted, what property rights protect it, and what regulatory structure exists to prevent a monopoly from strip-mining a commons. We have reasonably good answers to these questions for oil, spectrum, and water. We have almost none for the attention of a teenager holding a phone at eleven at night.

The metaphor was never a metaphor. We just haven't built the institutions the market implies we need.
